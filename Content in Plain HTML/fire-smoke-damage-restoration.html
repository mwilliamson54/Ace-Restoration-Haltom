After a fire, make sure the property is safe before arranging cleanup

Do not re-enter a fire-damaged building until the fire department or appropriate authority has said it is safe. A structure may have weakened floors, ceilings, roof components, stairs, electrical systems, or other hazards that are not obvious from the doorway. Hot spots may remain, and smoke residues or damaged contents can create additional exposure. If you smell gas, see downed electrical equipment, or notice structural movement, keep away and contact the appropriate emergency service or utility.

Once authorities have released the property for appropriate access, avoid rushing into broad cleaning. Wiping soot with an ordinary cloth can smear residue or drive it into porous surfaces. Running fans or central HVAC may move smoke particles into areas that were less affected. Do not use household chemicals on unknown residues or mix cleaning products. A professional assessment can identify the affected surfaces and discuss which cleaning methods are appropriate for each material.

If the fire has also damaged the roof, windows, doors, or other openings, temporary protection may be needed to reduce exposure to weather or unauthorized entry. The work should be performed safely and by qualified people. Structural repair, electrical work, roofing, and permitted construction may require separate specialist contractors.

Smoke damage can spread beyond the room where the fire began

Smoke moves through openings and air pathways. Depending on the building layout and ventilation, residue and odor may affect hallways, bedrooms, closets, ceilings, furnishings, and contents in rooms that did not experience direct flame. The extent can vary with the materials burned, fire temperature, duration, ventilation, and firefighting activity. A room that looks clean may still have an odor or fine residue on surfaces.

Assessment should consider the whole affected area rather than only the charred materials. Different surfaces respond differently to smoke and soot. Painted drywall, unfinished wood, tile, fabrics, electronics, insulation, and stored belongings each have their own cleaning and restoration limits. The objective is to identify what can be cleaned, what needs specialist handling, and what cannot reasonably be restored.

Soot removal requires the right method for the surface

Soot is a residue produced by incomplete combustion. Its appearance and behavior depend on the materials burned and conditions during the fire. Some residue may be dry and powdery; other residue may be oily or sticky. Applying water or the wrong cleaning product can make some soot harder to remove. Scrubbing can spread particles or damage the finish. That is why a restoration plan should identify the residue and surface before selecting a method.

Walls and ceilings may require different treatment from hard floors, metal fixtures, cabinetry, upholstery, or contents. Porous materials can hold residue more deeply than sealed surfaces. Some items may be candidates for specialist contents cleaning; others may need to be discarded based on contamination, heat exposure, or damage. Do not assume that a single cleaner or technique is suitable for every room.

Kitchen fires: a small origin can create a larger cleanup area

Kitchen fires can involve cooking oils, food, plastics, cabinetry, appliances, and other household materials. Smoke may move into adjoining living spaces, while heat can affect cabinets, range hoods, ceilings, and nearby finishes. Fire suppression may introduce water that affects lower cabinets, flooring, or rooms below. A kitchen restoration assessment should therefore consider flame damage, smoke residue, odor, contents, and secondary water damage rather than focusing only on the stove or range.

Do not switch appliances back on or use electrical equipment that may have been affected by heat, water, or smoke until it has been checked by an appropriately qualified professional. Avoid using a household vacuum on soot or ash unless the equipment is designed and approved for that material and situation. Keep children and pets away from the affected area.

Firefighting water can create a second restoration problem

Water used to extinguish a fire may enter floors, walls, cabinets, insulation, and lower levels. The water may be mixed with soot, debris, or other residues, so it should not automatically be treated like a clean plumbing leak. A property can need both fire and water damage restoration. The work may involve removing accessible water, assessing affected materials, drying where appropriate, and cleaning residues using methods suited to the condition.

In a multi-story building, firefighting water may travel down through ceiling penetrations or service openings. In a commercial property, water can affect inventory, machinery, stockrooms, and adjacent tenant spaces. The restoration plan should map both the fire-related damage and the water-related damage. Emergency board-up and temporary property protection

A fire can leave broken windows, damaged doors, roof openings, or other exposed areas. Temporary board-up or weather protection may help reduce additional damage and limit unauthorized access while permanent repairs are planned. The right method depends on the opening, building condition, access, weather, and safety requirements. Do not climb onto a damaged roof, work around unstable masonry, or attempt to secure a structure that has not been released for safe access.

Temporary protection is not the same as permanent reconstruction. A board-up may secure an opening, but it does not repair the roof, replace a damaged window system, or restore a compromised structural component. The scope should make clear what temporary work is being performed and what follow-up trade or permit work may be necessary.

Cleaning and restoring fire-damaged contents

Contents can include furniture, clothing, documents, electronics, household items, stock, office equipment, and personal keepsakes. Whether an item can be restored depends on heat exposure, residue, material type, odor, contamination, and the cleaning methods available. Some belongings may be suitable for specialist cleaning; others may be damaged beyond practical restoration. Electronics and important documents may require specialist handling rather than ordinary wiping or drying.

Make an inventory and take photographs where it is safe to do so. Keep damaged items available for insurer inspection when requested, but do not store unsafe or contaminated items in a way that exposes occupants. Ask the insurer how it wants items documented and whether it needs to inspect them before disposal. A contents list should include a description, location, condition, and any purchase details you can reasonably provide.

Our fire-damaged contents cleaning page discusses how contents decisions fit into the wider restoration plan. Do not assume that every item can be cleaned or that every item must be discarded; the decision is specific to the loss and the item.

Residential fire and smoke restoration

For a family home, the priority is to understand which rooms are safe, what belongings can be protected, and how to plan the work without exposing occupants to unsafe conditions. A fire in a kitchen, garage, utility room, or bedroom can affect different parts of the property. Smoke may move into closets and soft furnishings; firefighting water may affect flooring; and damaged openings may need temporary protection. The restoration scope should explain which rooms are included and whether the family can safely occupy any part of the home.

Do not return children, pets, or vulnerable occupants to affected areas until the responsible authorities and qualified professionals have addressed the relevant safety concerns. If temporary accommodation is being considered, speak with your insurer about any applicable policy provisions. Keep copies of expenses and communications, but do not assume a particular expense will be reimbursed without insurer confirmation.

Commercial fire and smoke restoration

For a business, fire damage can affect employees, customers, inventory, machinery, records, computers, and the ability to operate. A retail unit may need to protect stock and customer access; an office may need to safeguard records and equipment; and a warehouse may have large storage areas, high ceilings, or specialist systems. The restoration plan should identify who controls access, what areas are restricted, and which operations can continue safely.

Building managers and business owners should coordinate with the fire authority, insurer, landlord, tenants, and any relevant safety or regulatory bodies. Food businesses may need to consider food disposal and health requirements. Industrial sites may need specialist advice about process materials or equipment. Restoration cleaning should not begin until the site is safe and the scope is understood.

How the fire and smoke restoration project is organized

  1. Emergency and safety status: Confirm that the fire authority has released the property for the proposed work and identify hazards.
  2. Initial discussion: Record the address, type of building, fire origin if known, visible damage, smoke movement, and water from firefighting.
  3. Assessment: Review affected surfaces, contents, odor, structural concerns, and areas that may need specialist inspection.
  4. Scope and coordination: Explain cleaning, contents handling, temporary protection, water mitigation, and separate repair or trade work.
  5. Cleaning and mitigation: Carry out the agreed work using methods appropriate to the residue and materials.
  6. Review and documentation: Explain completed work, remaining issues, and any reconstruction or specialist steps.

The sequence is not identical for every fire. A small cooking incident, a garage fire, and a larger commercial loss have different hazards and requirements. Our restoration process guide describes how we approach the wider project lifecycle.

Insurance questions after a fire

Contact your insurer promptly and ask how to report the loss, what documentation is required, and whether temporary protection or emergency mitigation needs authorization. Take photographs after the property is safe to enter, make an inventory of damaged belongings, and keep receipts for temporary repairs. Do not throw away damaged items before asking whether the adjuster needs to inspect them, unless they pose a safety or sanitation risk.

Coverage depends on the policy, cause of loss, exclusions, limits, and insurer's investigation. Fire, smoke, contents, temporary housing, debris removal, and water from firefighting may be handled under different provisions. Ask the insurer to explain the applicable policy language. Frequently asked questions about fire and smoke damage restoration

Should I clean smoke residue before the restoration inspection?

It is generally better to document the condition and ask for guidance before attempting broad cleaning. Some residues smear or become harder to remove when treated incorrectly. Do not enter until the property is released as safe.

Can smoke affect clothing and furniture?

Yes. Soft goods and porous contents may retain odor or residue. Whether an item can be restored depends on its material, exposure, and available cleaning methods. Keep an inventory and ask about specialist contents handling.

Will you repair the roof or rebuild the house?

Temporary protection and restoration cleanup may be part of the response, but permanent roofing, structural, electrical, and reconstruction work can require separate specialists, permits, or inspections. Confirm the exact scope before authorizing work.

Does firefighting water need separate cleanup?

It may. Water can affect building materials and contents, and its condition may be influenced by soot and debris. The restoration assessment should consider both fire-related residue and water-related moisture.
