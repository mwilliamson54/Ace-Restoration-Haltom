Mold growth is a moisture problem first

Mold is part of the natural environment. Spores can enter buildings through doors, windows, ventilation, clothing, and ordinary dust. Their presence alone does not tell you whether a building has an active mold problem. Growth becomes more likely when spores land on a surface that remains damp long enough and has suitable material to support it. Drying the building and correcting the moisture source are therefore central to lasting mold control.

In a home, moisture may come from a plumbing leak, roof opening, overflowing fixture, appliance hose, condensation, poor ventilation, or water that remained in materials after an earlier incident. In a commercial building, leaks from shared plumbing, roof assemblies, mechanical systems, cooling equipment, or exterior drainage can affect ceilings, wall cavities, storage rooms, and occupied spaces. A visible spot may be the first sign of a larger moisture condition, but it is not proof of how far the problem extends.

Do not rely on color to identify a particular mold species or judge its danger. Mold can appear in different colors and textures, and appearance alone cannot determine species or health risk. If growth is extensive, recurring, hidden, or associated with a significant water event, seek an appropriate professional assessment rather than guessing based on photographs.

Mold removal and mold remediation: what is the difference?

People often use “mold removal,” “mold cleanup,” and “mold remediation” interchangeably. In everyday conversation, all may refer to addressing mold growth and the moisture conditions behind it. In Texas, however, mold assessment and mold remediation are regulated activities with defined roles. Assessment generally involves inspection or surveying to provide information about mold presence and, where required, a remediation protocol. Remediation involves the removal, cleaning, sanitizing, or other treatment of mold or mold-contaminated matter.

Where Texas licensing rules apply, the assessment and remediation roles must be kept separate on the same project. A person or company may hold credentials for both types of work, but that does not authorize them to perform both regulated activities on the same project where the law prohibits it. The appropriate licensed mold assessment professional and licensed mold remediation professional must each work within their own role. Ask who is responsible for the assessment, who will perform remediation, and what documents will be provided.

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) maintains the Mold Assessors and Remediators program. Its guidance explains licensing, exemptions, project documentation, and the restriction on performing both assessment and remediation on the same project. Review the current information at TDLR Mold Assessors and Remediators and its frequently asked questions. Project requirements depend on the property, area, occupancy, and applicable exemptions; this page is general information, not a legal determination.

What a mold-related project may involve

A careful project begins by understanding the moisture history and the areas of concern. A qualified assessor may inspect visible conditions, consider building materials and moisture pathways, and determine whether sampling or a written remediation protocol is appropriate. Testing is not automatically necessary in every situation. The need for sampling depends on the question being asked and whether the results will help guide a decision. A laboratory result without a meaningful inspection and building context can be difficult to interpret.

Where a regulated project requires a protocol, the protocol should identify the work areas, materials to be removed or cleaned, protective equipment, containment approach, remediation methods, and clearance criteria. The remediation contractor should explain how the proposed work follows that project-specific plan. If the work area changes after opening a wall or ceiling, the responsible professionals may need to reassess the scope rather than continuing beyond the documented plan without appropriate direction.

Remediation may include establishing containment, protecting adjacent areas, using suitable personal protective equipment, controlling dust and debris, removing materials that cannot be effectively cleaned, cleaning remaining surfaces, and addressing the moisture source. The exact method depends on the materials, amount of growth, access, and project requirements. A visible patch is not the whole project if moisture remains behind it.

After remediation, the project may require a post-remediation assessment or clearance process under the applicable rules and protocol. The person performing that assessment must be appropriately licensed and independent of the remediation role as required. Ask for the applicable reports and any required Certificate of Mold Damage Remediation when one is issued.

Common places where indoor mold is discovered

Bathrooms and laundry areas

Bathrooms generate moisture through showers, baths, sinks, and ventilation. Failed caulk, plumbing connections, slow leaks under vanities, and damp materials around tubs can create concealed conditions. Laundry rooms have supply hoses, drain connections, washers, and sometimes water heaters. A recurring musty odor or repeated staining deserves attention to the moisture source, not just a surface wipe. See our bathroom mold removal page.

Attics and roof assemblies

Roof leaks can wet decking, insulation, framing, and ceiling materials. Condensation may also occur when warm, moist indoor air reaches a colder surface or when ventilation and air sealing are not functioning as intended. An attic inspection must be performed safely; do not walk on ceiling joists or disturb insulation without understanding the hazards. Learn more about attic mold removal.

Crawlspaces and enclosed lower areas

Moisture in a crawlspace may relate to plumbing leaks, ground moisture, drainage, ventilation, or water entering through openings. The conditions can affect floor framing and the indoor environment above. A crawlspace may have limited access, electrical equipment, pests, or other hazards. Do not enter a confined or unsafe area to investigate mold yourself. Our crawlspace mold remediation guide explains the considerations.

Walls, ceilings, and cabinets

Leaks from plumbing, roofs, windows, or appliances can wet drywall, insulation, baseboards, cabinet backs, and framing. Growth may be concealed behind a finish, so a musty smell or recurring discoloration should prompt a moisture investigation. Do not cut open a wall or ceiling without checking for electrical wiring and potentially hazardous building materials. See black mold removal for information about visible dark growth and why color is not a species test.

Why the moisture source must be corrected

Removing visible growth without fixing the underlying water condition can lead to recurrence. A plumbing leak may need repair by a licensed plumber. Roof leaks may require a qualified roofer. Condensation may call for changes to ventilation, insulation, air sealing, or HVAC operation, depending on the building. Drainage problems may require a site-specific solution. The remediation scope should identify which work is included and which repairs need another professional.

After water damage, drying should be addressed promptly. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency emphasizes moisture control as the key to mold control and recommends drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours where possible. That timeframe is a practical prevention target, not a guarantee that mold will or will not develop at a particular hour. Conditions vary with temperature, material, water source, and how long a surface remains damp. Visit our water damage restoration hub for extraction and structural drying information.

If mold appears after a leak, the restoration work may involve both water-damage mitigation and a separate mold assessment/remediation process. The two should be coordinated without blurring regulated roles. Our page on mold after water damage explains that connection.

Safety: what property owners should and should not do

Keep children, pets, and occupants away from visibly affected or actively damp areas while the situation is being evaluated. Do not dry-brush, sand, scrape, or aggressively disturb extensive growth. Avoid using ordinary fans in a way that could spread dust from an affected area into clean rooms. Never mix bleach with ammonia, acids, or other cleaning products. Do not paint or caulk over visible mold and assume the issue is resolved.

Small areas on hard, non-porous surfaces may sometimes be cleaned by an owner under appropriate conditions, but that does not mean every project is a do-it-yourself task. Porous materials such as drywall, carpet, ceiling tile, and insulation can be difficult to clean fully once mold growth is established. The EPA notes that moldy porous materials may need to be discarded. Extensive growth, significant water damage, contaminated water, hidden growth, or people with heightened sensitivity in the building are reasons to seek professional guidance.

Do not disturb materials that may contain asbestos or lead-based paint. Older buildings and previous renovations can contain hazardous materials that require specialist assessment before demolition or sanding. If water is near electrical systems, do not enter or touch equipment. Resolve electrical and structural hazards before work begins.

Mold services for homes, rental properties, and businesses

Residential projects may involve a single bathroom, attic, bedroom wall, cabinet area, or a larger moisture event affecting multiple rooms. The work plan should take account of household access, belongings, ventilation, pets, and whether the family can safely occupy unaffected parts of the home. A landlord or property manager may need to coordinate access, tenant communication, maintenance records, and responsibility for repairs. The assessment and remediation requirements do not disappear simply because a property is rented.

Commercial projects can involve offices, retail units, warehouses, schools, and multi-tenant buildings. Shared roofs, plumbing risers, HVAC equipment, and common walls can complicate the source investigation. Building managers should identify who can authorize work, what areas must remain open, and which occupants need notice. A commercial remediation plan may also need to coordinate with facility operations and other contractors.

In Texas, licensing exemptions can depend on factors such as the size of the affected area, type of property, owner involvement, and other conditions in the statute and rules. Do not assume an exemption applies based only on a quick visual estimate. TDLR's current rules and FAQs should be consulted for the specific project.

Questions about mold remediation

Does every mold problem require laboratory testing?

No. Testing is not automatically required before cleanup or remediation. A qualified assessor can explain whether sampling answers a specific question for the building and project. Visual inspection, moisture investigation, and building history are important context.

Can the same company inspect for mold and remediate it?

Texas allows a person or company to hold credentials in both areas if requirements are met, but the law restricts performing both regulated assessment and remediation activities on the same project, subject to limited statutory exceptions. Confirm the responsible licensed parties before work begins.

Can mold simply be painted over?

No. Painting over mold does not correct moisture or remove the growth. The affected surface must be addressed appropriately and the source of moisture corrected before finishes are restored.

Will all mold spores be removed from my home?

No building can be made entirely free of ordinary environmental mold spores. The goal is to address active indoor growth, remove or clean affected materials as appropriate, and correct the moisture conditions that support it.

Does insurance pay for mold remediation?

Coverage depends on the policy, cause of moisture, exclusions, endorsements, and claim decision. Ask your insurer to explain the applicable terms. Our insurance claims help page covers documentation and claim communication.
